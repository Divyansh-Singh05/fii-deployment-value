# 7 · Conclusion

We took a fourteen-year, 25.2-million-trade transaction record of foreign
institutional flow — transaction-level, entity-attributed, of a class that does
not normally leave regulators — and asked what it is worth.

The statistical question was never in doubt. Every one of seven applications
produced an effect that cleared conventional inference and replicated on an
out-of-sample era covering a substantially different cross-section. On the
standard by which the replication literature judges published work, all seven
pass.

Six of them then fail, at four different levels, to reach a use. One is not
knowable at the lag a decision requires. Two are already contained in the
baseline they would improve — and one of those two is the same predictor that
passes against a simpler baseline, which is the sharpest available statement
that incremental value is a property of the pair rather than of the data. Two
lie below the resolution of the metric a user would consult, one of them the
conditioning mechanism that the system carrying it is named for. One is
consumed by the cost of acting on it, at a breakeven roughly half a realistic
institutional cost.

One survives: the record aggregated to market level, at a deployable lag,
against a baseline that does not already contain it, worth about two per cent of
capital at matched coverage. Its conditions are exactly the ones the six
failures violate.

A control predictor drawn from outside the dataset, more era-stable than
anything the record produced, dies at the same level and by the same mechanism.
The friction is in the transition from statistical evidence to institutional
use, not in this data.

Three things follow that we would defend beyond this case.

**The binding constraint is a property of the use.** Six failures at four
levels means no single friction explains the pattern, and no sentence about
"whether this dataset carries information" is well posed without naming what it
is to be used for. A breadth design cannot see this, because it holds the
friction fixed.

**The decisive checks are cheap and are not the ones the field argues about.**
Re-running a regression at a deployable lag costs a line. Scoring against a
better-specified baseline rather than a weak one costs a model. Comparing
against a trivial rule, paired on the events both act upon, costs a join.
Ablating a mechanism costs a re-run of machinery already written. Each of those
killed an application here that no amount of additional data, sample length or
model capacity would have touched.

**Comparison establishes that a system is better; only ablation establishes
why.** Our risk density beats its benchmark decisively in the deep tail, and
the mechanism it is named for contributes one part in eleven thousand. Both
statements are true and only the second is informative about the data. The
practice is routine in machine learning and close to absent from risk-model
validation, and on this evidence its absence is expensive.

Finally, a remark about the paper's own numbers. Applying the provenance
protocol to the source programme found two pre-registered gating regressions
with no producing stage, a metrics table quoted from a copy six weeks behind its
origin, and a stage whose reported effect size was a third larger than its
current object supports because it was never re-executed after an audit. None of
those changed a verdict. All of them changed a number that had already been
written down. We report them because a study of the distance between a
statistic and its usable content is not entitled to a weaker standard for its
own arithmetic than it demands of the finding.
