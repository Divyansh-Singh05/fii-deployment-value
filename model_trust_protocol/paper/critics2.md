# Pre-Submission Peer Review: "From Statistical Evidence to Decision Value: Six Deployment Constraints on an Institutional Flow Dataset" — Assessed for the *Indian Journal of Finance*

## TL;DR
- **As written, this manuscript will almost certainly be desk-rejected or rejected-after-review at the *Indian Journal of Finance (IJF)*** — publishability is ~5–10% as-is. The mismatch is structural: IJF is an ABDC-C, Scopus (SJR Q3) practitioner-and-academic monthly whose staple is ≤6,000-word OLS/GARCH/ARDL/panel empirical papers with clear positive findings and managerial implications, while this is a 22-page, single-author, methodology/meta-science paper whose headline result is that six of seven applications *failed*, built on machinery (filtered HMM regime layers, discrete-time hazard models, CRPS/proper-scoring-rule density evaluation, gradient-boosted feature blocks, 106 walk-forward vintages) far above the journal's typical readership.
- **The science is genuinely strong and the dataset is rare** (25.16m trades, masked participant IDs, depository-settlement FII data 2011–2025) — but the paper is over-disclosed, under-tabulated, and mis-venued. After deep revision and simplification it could reach ~35–45% at IJF; however, the author's *dominant* strategy is to submit the rigorous version to a better-fit outlet — **Journal of Emerging Market Finance (ABDC-A), IIMB Management Review (ABDC-B), Journal of Asset Management (ABDC-A), or Finance Research Letters / International Review of Financial Analysis (ABDC-A)** — where a negative-result methodology paper is publishable and the quant machinery is understood.
- **The most damaging single technical flaw is the transaction-cost claim**: the paper's 7.33–7.41 bps breakeven and 15 bps assumed cost are not survivable for Indian *delivery cash equity*, where Securities Transaction Tax alone is 0.10% (10 bps) per side — kept unchanged in Budget 2026 while F&O rates were hiked effective 1 April 2026. Unless the strategy trades futures/intraday (which must be stated explicitly), the execution-constraint result is mis-specified — but this actually *strengthens* the paper's own thesis (the strategy fails execution by an even wider margin than reported).

**Transaction-cost reality (decisive).** For Indian *delivery cash equity*, one-way explicit costs are floored at ~11–12 bps because STT alone is 0.10% = 10 bps per side (per CBDT/Budget 2026 via ClearTax: "0.1% on the price at which equity share is purchased" and "0.1% on the price at which the equity share is sold," left unchanged in Budget 2026 while F&O STT rose from 1 April 2026 — futures 0.02%→0.05%, options 0.10%→0.15%). Add stamp duty 0.015%/1.5 bps buy-side, NSE exchange charge ~0.30 bps, SEBI fee ~0.01 bps, GST on brokerage+charges, and institutional brokerage ~2–5 bps (ICRA notes cash-segment brokerage historically 4–7 bps, trending toward ~2 bps). Adding market impact — NSE India's official Nifty 50 page states "Impact cost of the Nifty 50 for a portfolio size of Rs.50 lakhs is 0.02% for the month March 2026," rising toward the 0.50% index-inclusion ceiling (per NSE Indices' "NIFTY Equity Indices" methodology: a stock must have "traded at an average impact cost of 0.50% or less during the last six months for 90% of the observations, for the basket size of Rs. 10 crore") — a realistic **all-in one-way cost for large-cap Indian cash equity is ~15–20 bps (range 13–25)**. Therefore: **the assumed 15 bps is reasonable-but-optimistic (not conservative), and the 7.33–7.41 bps breakeven is unattainable for delivery cash equity** — STT alone exceeds it by ~35%. This does not weaken the paper; it means the execution constraint bites even harder than claimed. The one exception: if the book trades index/stock **futures** (post-Budget-2026 STT 0.05% = 5 bps sell-side, no delivery STT/stamp) or **intraday** (STT 0.025% = 2.5 bps sell-side only), ~7 bps is conceivable — so the manuscript *must state the instrument and cost stack explicitly* and present a cost-grid sensitivity table rather than a single assumed figure.

## Details

### 1. Journal Fit & Scope Alignment
Thematically the paper is in-scope (FII flow, volatility, derivatives-adjacent risk forecasting, Indian markets). The problem is register and payload, not topic. IJF's readership — academics, PhD scholars, and finance practitioners — is served by papers that state a hypothesis, run a recognizable estimator on public NSE/BSE data, and end with actionable managerial implications. This manuscript instead asks an epistemological question ("how far does statistical content carry once the tests stop being statistical?") and answers it with a six-constraint attrition framework and an admittedly negative distribution of outcomes. The advanced machinery — filtered HMM regime labels, a discrete-time hazard model of episode termination, predictive densities scored by CRPS-type integrated proper scoring rules, gradient-boosted participant-composition feature blocks, ablation studies, and 106 monthly walk-forward vintages — is not the kind IJF's referees routinely evaluate, and the paper does little to translate it for a broad audience. **Mismatch risk is high and largely irreducible without a rewrite that (a) leads with the one surviving result as a positive managerial finding, (b) demotes the failures to a robustness narrative, and (c) strips or appendixes the heaviest machinery.** Even then, the length ceiling and exhibit-simplicity norm bind hard.

### 2. Originality & Academic Contribution
The genuine novelty is the **inversion**: applying six ordered deployment constraints to *one dataset at seven points in a modelling stack*, rather than the field-standard of testing one constraint (e.g., transaction costs, or out-of-sample stability) across many predictors. Framed against Hou-Xue-Zhang and Jensen-Kelly-Pedersen — who ask "how many factors survive?" — this paper asks "how far does one data source survive down the deployment chain?" That is a legitimate, publishable reframing, and the **external-control design (a foreign index return predicting domestic realized volatility, which is more era-stable than anything the FII record produced yet still fails at competition)** is a genuinely elegant identification of where the attrition occurs — at the evaluation-to-deployment transition, not in the data. This is the paper's strongest single idea and deserves to be foregrounded.

Two caveats. First, several of the six constraints are **relabelings of well-known ideas** (availability = look-ahead/lag realism; competition = incremental value over baseline; execution = transaction-cost adjustment; detectability = economic vs. statistical significance). The novelty is the *ordering and unified application*, not the individual constraints — the paper must claim the former and not overclaim the latter. Second, the **verification/provenance protocol** (every reported figure bound to an artifact, digest, and extractor; three pre-existing errors caught, including a stale metrics table and an inflated effect size) is a real and unusually honest contribution to research-integrity practice, but it reads as engineering rather than finance and will land better in a meta-science or data-science venue than at IJF.

**The most under-exploited asset is the dataset itself**: 25,155,785 trades across 5,960 instruments, 2011–2025, from depository settlement data, with masked participant IDs — a genuinely rare resource for Indian-market microstructure research and, reframed as a data paper or an FII-informativeness study, far more publishable than the meta-science framing.

### 3. Methodological Rigor — referee objections
A hostile-but-fair referee would raise the following; several already have answers in the manuscript that need promotion rather than new work:

- **Forward-looking 60-day-within-era instrument selection (10.8% of universe).** The "bounded exposure" defence is partially adequate but a strictly causal (purely backward-looking) robustness re-selection is needed to close the look-ahead objection. Currently conceded, not tested — a referee will demand the test.
- **"Replication" labelling.** Only 441 of 1,028 instruments are common to both eras; the paper concedes this is *era stability*, not replication. **Figure 2 labelling constraint 1 as "Replication" is misleading and a referee will demand it be renamed** (e.g., "Era Stability / Holdout Persistence"). Cheap, mandatory fix.
- **Masked-identifier non-persistence.** IDs re-minted monthly (median lifetime 1 month; 363,663 FII identifiers) is a serious data-integrity finding that is under-prominent. It undercuts any cross-day entity interpretation and **directly threatens the participant-composition results** (the 74.8 bps quintile spread, constraint 4): within-day-only statistics may not measure the persistent "participant type" the feature block implicitly assumes. This needs to be elevated from a validation aside to a first-order caveat on the constraint-4 results.
- **z² as dependent variable (Eq. 1).** Squared standardized returns are highly non-normal, heteroskedastic, and right-skewed; OLS-with-date-clustering is fragile. Date fixed effects absorb market-wide variation, so market-level variables can enter only as interactions — a point the paper should state explicitly. **Driscoll–Kraay or two-way-cluster + block-bootstrap SEs should be the primary specification, not a footnote**, given strong serial correlation in volatility. The paper's robustness rows (−5.20 two-way clustered; −6.47 stationary block bootstrap; lagged row stable at −0.42 to −0.52) are reassuring and should be promoted into the main table.
- **AUC-only hazard evaluation, no calibration.** Conceded, but a referee will insist on a reliability diagram, Brier-score decomposition, or calibration curve. Discrimination without calibration is insufficient for a forecast the paper then costs in bps. Mandatory.
- **The "−2.0 score differential" gate is never defined.** This reads like a Diebold–Mariano or Giacomini–White test statistic but is not named or given a null/asymptotic distribution. **This is a serious specification gap** — the central pass/fail criterion of the whole paper is an unnamed statistic. Must be formally defined.
- **Multiple testing / data snooping.** The paper concedes the four engine gates were a *sequential search* (not a fixed family) and that eight further strategy books were run and unpriced. A narrative concession is not enough: a formal **White Reality Check, Hansen SPA, or Deflated Sharpe Ratio (Bailey & López de Prado)** correction is required, especially since the sole survivor's one-sided p=0.0095 already degrades to 0.038 (Holm) / 0.052 (common window) — i.e., it is one honest correction away from failing.
- **Expected shortfall via coverage ratio, not a formal backtest.** Conceded; Acerbi-Székely (2014) or Du-Escanciano (2017) should be applied, particularly because the surviving result is a *centre-weighted* density score, which explicitly does not certify the far tail a risk manager consumes.
- **TWFE bias under staggered episode timing.** Conceded and uncorrected. The "bounded exposure" argument (episode-level estimation enters only Table 4) is reasonable *if* Table 4's effects are not load-bearing — but they are among the reported findings, so at minimum a de Chaisemartin–D'Haultfœuille or Callaway–Sant'Anna robustness check on the episode design is warranted.
- **Economically trivial effect sizes.** Table 4 score effects of ±0.00005 on a base of ~0.55 (≈1 part in 11,000) are statistically detectable but economically meaningless; reporting them as "findings" strains credibility and, ironically, *proves the paper's own detectability point*. The paper should frame these explicitly as demonstrations of the statistical-vs-economic gap, not as results.
- **Sample-size-driven significance.** With 577,245 instrument-days the paper concedes significance is "close to automatic." This genuinely undercuts constraint 0 (Existence) as a meaningful hurdle — the author should own this and argue that this is *precisely why* constraints 2–5 matter, turning a weakness into the thesis.
- **Proprietary, machine-local, non-shareable data.** For a Scopus-indexed journal in 2026 this is a real but survivable weakness. IJF has no strong open-data mandate (unlike *Journal of Finance*/RFS, which run data editors and DCAS-compliant replication packages), so it is less fatal here than at a top-tier outlet — but the author should provide a synthetic/simulated reproducibility harness, a data-access statement, and code (even if run on masked/synthetic inputs) to signal good faith.
- **External validity.** n=1 market, 1 dataset, 1 author, 1 external control. The "six constraints" framework is being pitched as portable; the paper's limitations section hedges this well ("a fact about 7 applications of 1 record in 1 market"). Keep the hedge; do not oversell portability.

### 4. Synthesis & Presentation
The literature review's six-part structure (Replication and Credibility; Constraints Between Significance and Use; Evaluating Forecasts/Densities/Backtests; Alternative Data and Its Valuation; FII Flow in India; Research Gap; Objectives) is logically ordered and the gap statement follows from it, though it is dense. **Figure 2 (the attrition funnel) is the single most important exhibit** and should be the paper's centerpiece — but it must be recolored greyscale for IJF and relabelled (drop "Replication"). **The four tables do not adequately support seven applications** — several applications live only in prose. **The single most obvious missing exhibit is a summary table listing all seven applications by name against their binding constraint** (Existence / Stability / Availability / Competition / Detectability / Execution); this should be added regardless of venue. The prose is extremely compressed — a broad IJF readership will struggle. The conclusion **over-discloses**: it volunteers so many limitations (uncorrected TWFE, unpriced books, no ES backtest, no calibration, machine-local reproducibility) that it hands a reviewer a ready-made rejection checklist. This is a genuine strategic tension: the honesty is admirable and correct, but at IJF specifically it invites rejection. The fix is not to hide flaws but to *resolve* the cheap ones (rename, add calibration, define the gate statistic, add the DSR correction) so that the residual disclosed limitations are clearly second-order. The title is accurate but abstract; for IJF it should foreground the Indian FII application. The structured abstract already conforms to IJF's Purpose/Design/Findings/Practical-Implications/Originality template — good.

## Recommendations

2. **[CRITICAL] Scope/register mismatch (meta-science, negative result).** A paper whose payload is "six of seven failed" fights IJF's required "Practical Implications" positive-finding orientation; high desk-reject and reject-after-review risk.
3. **[CRITICAL] Referee-competence gap.** IJF's reviewer pool is unlikely to contain referees fluent in CRPS/DM-GW density tests, HMM hazard layers, or Deflated Sharpe Ratios; the paper is likely to be misjudged or bounced as "not suitable for our readership."
4. **[HIGH] Exhibit complexity vs. greyscale/low-complexity norm.** The funnel and density-score tables violate IJF's "as low as possible" exhibit guidance.
5. **[HIGH] Undefined central test statistic (the −2.0 gate).** Any competent referee rejects a pass/fail framework whose key statistic is unnamed.
6. **[HIGH] Transaction-cost mis-specification (7.33–7.41 bps breakeven; 15 bps assumption).** For delivery cash equity, STT alone is 10 bps/side; a referee who knows Indian microstructure will flag this immediately.
7. **[HIGH] Multiple-testing not formally corrected.** Sequential-search gates + eight unpriced books, with the survivor already marginal under Holm — demands a formal SPA/Reality-Check/DSR correction.

12. **[LOW] Single-authored, no funding.** Not disqualifying at IJF (many IJF papers are single-authored), but combined with the above raises the "unusual submission" prior.

**Suggested reframed title (for IJF):** *"When Does Foreign Institutional Flow Data Improve Volatility Forecasts? Six Deployment Tests on 25 Million Trades in Indian Equities."*

**Suggested reframed abstract (IJF structured template):**
- *Purpose:* To determine how much of the statistically detectable content in transaction-level foreign-institutional flow data survives the constraints of real-world deployment in Indian equity risk management.
- *Design/Methodology/Approach:* Using 25.16 million depository-settlement trades in Indian equities (2011–2025), the study applies one flow dataset at seven points in a forecasting stack and subjects each to six ascending constraints — existence, era stability, availability at the decision lag, competition against a deployed baseline, economic detectability, and execution cost — under pre-registered thresholds and walk-forward validation.
- *Findings:* Only an aggregate, market-level net-flow variable, entered at a two-day reporting lag into a Student-t density forecast of realized volatility, survives all six constraints; stock-level and strategy applications fail at availability, competition, detectability, or execution.
- *Practical Implications:* Domestic risk managers and exchanges can extract usable volatility-forecast value from aggregate FII flow, but stock-selection and long-short uses are defeated by reporting lags and Indian execution costs; amid the FII-to-DII shift, the surviving variable should be re-estimated on domestic institutional flow.
- *Originality/Value:* The study inverts the replication literature — testing one dataset against many deployment constraints rather than many predictors against one — and demonstrates, on rare participant-attributed Indian microstructure data, that attrition occurs at the evaluation-to-deployment transition.
